# Introduction

[LD-score regression](https://www.nature.com/articles/ng.3211) is a commonly used method in complex trait genetics to estimate heritability and genetic correlation. The advantage of this method is that it does not require access to individual-level genotype data, and can estimate these parameters using only GWAS summary statistics. While LDSC is undoubtedly useful, its derivation is rather complex, which is why I wanted to make a blog post to explain how it works, and highlight some of its assumptions. This is based off of Sasha Gusev's [excellent derivation](http://gusevlab.org/projects/hsq/ldsc_deriv.pdf), which the curious reader can refer to for a more rigorous proof.

Okay, let's start by defining how a phenotype can be modelled for one person. An individual's genotype (DNA sequence) is represented as a vector, with each element denoting the number of alleles (0, 1, 2) for a given position:

\[
\mathbf{genotype} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}
\]

Each allele has a unique causal effect on the phenotype. So to figure out the genetic component of a phenotype, we need to multiply the alleles by their respective effects and add them up:

\[
\begin{align*}
y &= 
\begin{bmatrix}
  x_{1}  \\
  x_{2}  \\
  \vdots \\
  x_{m}
\end{bmatrix} \times
\begin{bmatrix}
  b_{1}  \\
  b_{2}  \\
  \vdots \\
  b_{m} 
\end{bmatrix} + e
\end{align*}
\]

Here, **y** represents the phenotype, **x** is a vector of genotypes (0, 1, 2), and **b** is a vector of causal effects. We multiply these vectors to get a weighted sum of trait-related alleles (conceptually similar to a polygenic risk score). The **e** represents random noise.
