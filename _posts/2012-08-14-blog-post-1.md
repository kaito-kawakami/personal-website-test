# Introduction

[LD-score regression](https://www.nature.com/articles/ng.3211) is a commonly used method in complex trait genetics to estimate heritability and genetic correlation. The advantage of this method is that it does not require access to individual-level genotype data, and can estimate these parameters using only GWAS summary statistics. While LDSC is undoubtedly useful, its derivation is rather complex, which is why I wanted to make a blog post to explain how it works, and highlight some of its assumptions. This is based off of Sasha Gusev's [excellent derivation](http://gusevlab.org/projects/hsq/ldsc_deriv.pdf), which the curious reader can refer to for a more rigorous proof.

Okay, let's start by defining how a phenotype can be modeled for one person. An individual's genotype (DNA sequence) is represented as a vector, with each element denoting the number of alleles (0, 1, 2) for a given position:

\[
\mathbf{genotype} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}
\]

Each allele has a unique causal effect on the phenotype. To compute the genetic component of the phenotype, we multiply the alleles by their respective effects and add them up. In other words, we calculate the dot product between the genotype vector and the effect vector:

\[
y = \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{b} + e
\]

where

\[
\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ \vdots \\ x_{m} \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} b_{1} \\ b_{2} \\ \vdots \\ b_{m} \end{pmatrix}.
\]

Here, **y** represents the phenotype, **\(\mathbf{x}\)** is the vector of genotypes (0, 1, 2), **\(\mathbf{b}\)** is the vector of causal effects, and **e** represents random noise. This formulation is conceptually similar to calculating a polygenic risk score.
