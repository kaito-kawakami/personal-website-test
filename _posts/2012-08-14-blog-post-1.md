# What is heritability, and why should we bother estimating it?
People are different. As scientists, we are interested in understanding sources of complex trait variation. Geneticists coined the term 'complex traits' to distinguish traits which are influenced by many genes of small effect, in constrast to Mendelian traits, which are determined by a single gene. Most traits we care about (height, BMI, behavior, etc.) are complex. We are interested in identifying sources of complex trait variation for two main reasons:

1. It helps us understand the trait better (basic research).
2. It helps to identify modifiable risk factors (epidemiology).

As a motivating example, let's say we wanted to investigate why people differ in height. If you measured the heights of 1000 people, you might end up with a distribution which looks something like this:

![Height Distribution](https://rafalab.dfci.harvard.edu/dsbook/book_files/figure-html/example-of-smoothed-density-1.png)

Where height (in inches) is plotted on the x-axis. We can see that mostly people are between 63 and 75 inches tall, however some people might be shorter or taller. One reason might be sex: on average, men are [5.5 inches taller than women](https://ourworldindata.org/cdn-cgi/imagedelivery/qLq-8BTgXU8yG0N6HnOy8g/4ce2ed5a-244c-4647-01d5-b17eec0d9200/w=1350). Another reason might be nutrition. 

One question geneticists are particularly interested in is how much of the differences between people in height can be attributed to genetics. This parameter, called **heritability**, is defined as:

$$
h^2 = \frac{\sigma^2_G}{\sigma^2_P}
$$

where $$\sigma^2_G$$ represents genetic variance, and $$\sigma^2_P$$ represents the total variance in the phenotype (genetics lingo for "trait"). In the case of height, it is about **80% heritable** ([source](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/twin-research-and-human-genetics/article/heritability-of-adult-body-height-a-comparative-study-of-twin-cohorts-in-eight-countries/3EF884AEA534C90F46F95C9FA3944C84)). So what does this mean? Is 80% of height determined by genetics? 

Well not so fast, heritability is simply a variance ratio, so if we decrease other sources of variance, this will increase heritability. For example, if we lived in a hypothetical world where everyone had the exact same upbringing, then the heritability of height would be 100%, because genetics would be the only way in which people differed. Does that mean genetics is the only thing which determines height? Obviously not. Heritability simply describes phenotypic variance attributable to genetics in one population, at one point in time (N.B heritability can change over time). Another way of thinking about this is plants on a windowsill. If you had three basil plants which had zero sunlight exposure, they would all be stunted and therefore have around the same height (low $$h^2$$). However, if they all recieve the same sunlight and water, then the resulting differences in plant height could be due to genetics (high $$h^2$$). 

So how do we estimate heritability? I'm going to cover a method called LD-score regression [LD-score regression](https://www.nature.com/articles/ng.3211) (LDSC) which uses GWAS summary data to estimate $$h^2$$. GWAS summary statistics are essentially just a list of SNPs with their marginal effect sizes on a phenotype, so extracting a heritability estimate requires some clever math. While LDSC is undoubtedly useful, its derivation is rather complex, which is why I wanted to make a blog post to explain how it works and highlight some of its assumptions. This is based on Sasha Gusev's [excellent derivation](http://gusevlab.org/projects/hsq/ldsc_deriv.pdf), which the curious reader can refer to for a more rigorous proof.

# Introduction to LDSC
Let's start by defining how a phenotype can be modeled for one person, from a genetics standpoint. An individual's genotype (DNA sequence) is represented as a vector, with each element denoting the number of alleles (0, 1, 2) for a given position:

$$
\mathbf{genotype} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix}
$$

Each allele has a unique causal effect on the phenotype. To compute the genetic component of a phenotype, we multiply the alleles by their respective effects and add them up. In other words, we calculate the dot product between the genotype vector and the effect vector:

$$
\Large y = \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{b} + e
$$

where:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ \vdots \\ x_{m} \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} b_{1} \\ b_{2} \\ \vdots \\ b_{m} \end{pmatrix}
$$

Here:
- **y** represents the phenotype,
- **x** is the vector of genotypes (0, 1, 2),
- **b** is the vector of causal effects,
- **e** represents random noise.

This is conceptually similar to calculating a **polygenic risk score** which is also a weighted sum of effect alleles. 

Since we typically collect DNA data on many people, $$\mathbf{x}$$ can be represented as a matrix, where rows represent alleles and columns represent individuals:

$$
\mathbf{X} =
\begin{bmatrix}
  \text{Person 1} & \text{Person 2} & \cdots \\
\hline
1 & 0 & \cdots \\
0 & 2 & \cdots \\
1 & 1 & \cdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Returning to our phenotype model, 

$$
\Large y = \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{b} + e
$$

How can we relate this equation to what we actually observe in GWAS? Well in GWSAS, For each SNP j, a GWAS provides an estimated marginal effect:

$$
\hat{\beta}_j = \frac{\mathbf{x}_j^T y}{N}.
$$
