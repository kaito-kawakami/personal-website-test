# What is heritability, and why should we bother estimating it?
People are different. As scientists, we are interested in understanding sources of complex trait variation for two main reasons:
1. It helps us understand the trait in question (basic research).
2. It helps to identify modifiable risk factors for the trait in question (epidemiology).

If we took 1000 people and measured their height, we might observe something like this:

![Height Distribution](https://rafalab.dfci.harvard.edu/dsbook/book_files/figure-html/example-of-smoothed-density-1.png)

In this example, most people are between 63 and 75 inches tall. However, some people are shorter or taller. One reason could be sex: on average, men are [14 cm taller than women](https://ourworldindata.org/cdn-cgi/imagedelivery/qLq-8BTgXU8yG0N6HnOy8g/4ce2ed5a-244c-4647-01d5-b17eec0d9200/w=1350). Another might be nutrition. However, one question geneticists are particularly interested in is how much of the differences between people in height can be attributed to genetics. This parameter, called **heritability**, is statistically defined as:

$$
h^2 = \frac{\sigma^2_G}{\sigma^2_P}
$$

where $ \sigma^2_G $ represents genetic variance, and $ \sigma^2_P $ represents the total variance in the phenotype (a fancy word geneticists use meaning "trait"). In the case of height, it is about **80% heritable** ([source](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/twin-research-and-human-genetics/article/heritability-of-adult-body-height-a-comparative-study-of-twin-cohorts-in-eight-countries/3EF884AEA534C90F46F95C9FA3944C84)).

# Introduction

[LD-score regression](https://www.nature.com/articles/ng.3211) is a commonly used method in complex trait genetics to estimate **heritability** and **genetic correlation**. The advantage of this method is that it does not require access to individual-level genotype data and can estimate these parameters using only **GWAS summary statistics**. While LDSC is undoubtedly useful, its derivation is rather complex, which is why I wanted to make a blog post to explain how it works and highlight some of its assumptions. This is based on Sasha Gusev's [excellent derivation](http://gusevlab.org/projects/hsq/ldsc_deriv.pdf), which the curious reader can refer to for a more rigorous proof.

Let's start by defining how a phenotype can be modeled for one person. An individual's genotype (DNA sequence) is represented as a vector, with each element denoting the number of alleles (0, 1, 2) for a given position:

$$
\mathbf{genotype} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix}
$$

Each allele has a unique causal effect on the phenotype. To compute the genetic component of the phenotype, we multiply the alleles by their respective effects and add them up. In other words, we calculate the dot product between the genotype vector and the effect vector:

$$
\Large y = \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{b} + e
$$

where:

$$
\mathbf{x} = \begin{pmatrix} x_{1} \\ x_{2} \\ \vdots \\ x_{m} \end{pmatrix} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} b_{1} \\ b_{2} \\ \vdots \\ b_{m} \end{pmatrix}
$$

Here:
- $ y $ represents the phenotype,
- $ \mathbf{x} $ is the vector of genotypes (0, 1, 2),
- $ \mathbf{b} $ is the vector of causal effects,
- $ e $ represents random noise.

This is conceptually similar to calculating a **polygenic risk score**. Since we typically collect DNA data on many people, $ \mathbf{x} $ can be represented as a matrix, where rows represent alleles and columns represent individuals:

$$
\mathbf{X} =
\begin{bmatrix}
  \text{Person 1} & \text{Person 2} & \cdots \\
\hline
1 & 0 & \cdots \\
0 & 2 & \cdots \\
1 & 1 & \cdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & 
\end{bmatrix}
$$
